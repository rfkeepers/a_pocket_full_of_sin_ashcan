# Bindings

It is not by choice that we fixate on another person. Doubtless the realization arrives by way of startling revelation. Waking to find yourself caught in the strictures of some compulsory emotion: desire or hatred or intrigue or awe or any other chord one might strike along your heartstrings. Make no mistake, whomever this person is, they did not arrest your fascination out of sympathy or cordial pleasure. They have a measure of control over you now.

A binding states how your character is caught up- emotionally or by some social contract- with another character. No one binding can adequately describe your relationship on the whole, nor are they intended for that purpose. They only spotlight a few important specifics, namely: how you feel about that other character, and what you may and may not do to them as a result.

## Writing bindings

Every binding is a terse, one-sided, descriptive statement about one character's feelings for another, plus the mechanically prescriptive constraints of those feelings. For example:

> Gaby is curious about Jack. She cannot **Barge in** on him.

The first name is the _bound_ character (Gaby), the second states which character holds the binding (Jack). Every other time you see a rule that states the effects of the binding, it's talking about the person bound by it (Gaby).

## Binding constraints

The elementary rule is that the bound character cannot perform any of the listed actions unto the character who holds the binding. Often- such as in the above example- you'll see this written plainly in the binding's text (ex: "She cannot..."). Even if it isn't stated so obviously, you can always assume the rule still holds.

Each binding can state up to two different constraints. Each constraint must name either a **play** or an **anchor**. The above example uses a basic play (Barge in). It strictly prohibits Gaby from using that play on Jack. That doesn't need to be taken literally; Gaby's player doesn't need to go to lengths to avoid interrupting Jack or getting in his way. But if she tries anything that could potentially trigger the play itself, that's when the binding takes hold. 

Setting a binding to a play gives it an intentionally tight focal point. If you're looking for something that allows the players a bit more room to explore the complexities in that relationship, you can try an anchor as well.

> Gaby is curious about Jack. She cannot **Barge in** on him, or _try to prove him wrong_.

This version extends the example with the anchor _Try to prove someone wrong_. With anchors, unlike plays, the players are expected to enforce the constraint anytime it comes up in the conversation, regardless of the context in which it appears.

Anchor bindings don't interact with the other rules about anchors. For example, when it comes to character sheets, anchors are normally tracked as being active or inactive. That status doesn't matter for a binding. The binding is what it is, and the state of that anchor is a separate matter entirely.

## Bindings and promises

On top of the other effects, all bindings impose one additional constraint regardless of how the binding is written. Anytime whomever holds the binding asks for a promise from the bound character, their request cannot be denied, unless the bound character takes a step toward `Adrift` to distance themselves from their feelings.

## Recording your bindings

When a new binding gets added, both characters involved must add it to their sheet. Remember, the sentiment is always one-sided (eg: Jack is not curious about Gaby) regardless of whose sheet it's on. It's important for both characters to write the binding the exact same, so that the binding holder isn't mixed up with the bound character.

The redundancy is there, yes, in part, for extra record-keeping to help everyone keep track of the details. But that's not all: having it on both sheets is also diegetic. The character on the other end is guaranteed some quantity of awareness to the emotions which transpire between them (perhaps, even, a very clear understanding). Both characters are encouraged to play at those emotions and constraints from their perspective ends; though, like many other mechanics, the player's are neither required nor expected to bring these statements to life through roleplay. The mechanical effects are there to help you make the statements count.

Since bindings are one-sided, the other character can also have the same binding (`Jack is curious about Gaby. He cannot Barge in on her.`). Or they might have a binding that complicates or antagonizes that sentiment (`Jack is uninterested in Gaby. He cannot Catch her eye.`).

One character can even have multiple different bindings with another character. This means Jack could be both _curious_ and _uninterested_ in Gaby. It's an unlikely combination, but not off limits. Regardless of the number of bindings on your sheet, or their sentiments, each one is a discrete thing, and the rules apply to them one at a time.

## Locking and breaking

Every binding has two stages: unlocked, and locked. Save for when the rules say otherwise, your new bindings always start off unlocked.

Some rules may tell you to either _lock_ or _break_ your bindings. When locking, an unlocked binding gets locked: circle it, underline it, or give it some other mark to signify the lock. If the binding is already locked, nothing happens. When breaking, a locked binding moves to unlocked, while an already unlocked binding gets removed from your sheet entirely.

## Advanced Bindings

You already know that bindings use plays and anchors for their constraints. In truth there's no actual rule about what you are and are not allowed to write for a constraint. We kept it to plays and anchors earlier only to simplify the world a little more. If you're just starting to play the game, it's still suggested that you stick with those rule alone. But if you're familiar with that already and looking for other ways to qualify your character's relationships, here's some ideas to chase.

Making up constraints on the fly ("she can never tell him a lie") is an obvious starting point. It's also an easy way to write a poor or ineffective binding. What you should try instead is to flip the requirements of the constraint, to make it a statement about what the bound character _must_ do, instead of what isn't allowed.

> Jack obeys Gaby. He must always follow her when she says "come along".
> Gaby desires Jack. She must **Catch his eye** at least once every session.
> Jack cherishes Gaby. Whenever he sees her **Revel**, he must **Draw her out** to learn more about her past.
