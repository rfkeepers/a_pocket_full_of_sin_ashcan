# About Moves

## The conversation

To explain moves we first need to explain conversations. Which is, as a notion and requirement, about as simple as you might be thinking.

Conversations happen the same inside the game as they do outside. You all chew the fat, sometimes taking hold of the spotlight to egoize, other times giving it up and staying quiet so that others can have their time. Mostly you take this opportunity with the people around you to share: ideas, conjectures, proposals, reactions, revelations, and so on.

Within the game you'll engage that way all the same. Only this time with your characters and the world squarely in the picture. If you have the spotlight you say what your character feels or what they do, then the MC or another player says how their character responds, you react in turn or let someone else have their say, and so on it goes until the spotlight switches.

This isn't metaphysics for its own sake: the conversation is _the game_. It's how you play. Now and then the rules will interject to guide you through a phase with strict mechanics and rigor and sequence. In those moments you will have to set aside the conversation to follow the rules. But for the rest, its all conversation.

## What's a move?

A move is the signal that right now something very important is about to happen. An event so significant that we couldn't simply talk about it. Conversing about the details- simply sharing what our character does or feels or thinks- wouldn't satisfy. The world must respond to this moment, must _change_ because of it. That's what moves do.

In their most elemental form all moves match a statement of what the character is about to do (or has already done) with rules about how the world must react in turn. The outcomes intentionally steer conversation to places of potent new opportunity. In short: "when I do it, this must happen as a result". Sometimes moves make you roll (or find some other resolution) to discover what actually changes; sometimes not. Sometimes you have to pay a price to make the move; most times not.

Equally important to understanding the quintessence of moves is understanding all the things moves are not. Moves are not tests of capability. Capability and opportunity are necessary to make moves in the first place. A move does not determine if you _can_. Your characters are sufficiently capable; if they can do it, use the move to find out what happens afterward.

Likewise, moves are not abilities. Ability for one implies inability for another. Any character can do the thing the move is talking about (or, at least, make an attempt). Even those moves that are yours and yours alone. What is truly yours is that certain outcome. That the move says you can do it, and that no one may argue otherwise, is a simple axiom to boot.

## Making moves

An old adage in the pbta community goes, "to do it, you do it". It's a neat way to say that the move tells you what you need to do (ex: **when you do that voodoo that you do...**), so you have to make your character do what it says. "What is that voodoo that I do?" You might ask? Well, you tell me. You're the one doing it. What matters is we all agree you're doing it.

Many moves provide plain and obvious triggers, but not all. Let's say a move triggers **when someone lies to you**. Do they have to tell you that they're lying? If so, when? Are you supposed to catch them in the act? If so, how? And what if you found out too late? Wait a second, is this really your move, or theirs? It's rare for a move to be _this_ messy. Let's take the opportunity to talk about it anyway.

First, it's not uncommon for moves to involve other characters. Participation can extend beyond whomever has the move, and that counts when hitting the trigger. Second, the players are not adversaries. Their characters? That's not out of the question. But not the players. A move like that, the invitation to reveal your character and their motives and secrets, it should be seen as a chance to share the spotlight, not a trap.

Lastly, there's the question of who is allowed to make moves. The players, most certainly. This game does something else which most pbta does not: the Mistress of Chimeras may let all the npcs in her menagerie make those same moves. Most commonly the obvious moves- she'll already have her hands full without needing to track the other moves in the game- but the possibility is always open.

## Calling your moves

Recall that play is primarily a conversation, and that moves highlight moments of significance. Play flows naturally between moves and conversation. In result, catching the right moment for a move does, at times, become a bit of an art. To do it, you do it, indeed. Your character will do many things during play and using moves is only one of them. There's a handful of ways for a move to slip through the cracks.

For example: if you perform the trigger for the move, are you _required_ to use the move, or can you elect to ignore the mechanics?

The question doesn't normally come up this way. In all likelihood you're already restlessly impatient to catch someone in a lie or do your voodoo; impatient to engage your moves. That's good, you should feel that way! Yet not every move is guaranteed to improve your situation, and dodging those moves can feel like dodging a bullet. That decision very clearly constitutes playing outside the intent and integrity of the game.

A more likely example: you all reflect a moment backward and realize someone had brushed against a trigger but didn't use their move. Are you breaking the game, did you do something wrong? Not precisely. Missing a move means you've weakened the moment, not prevented it altogether.

In the ideal table, everyone all participants strive to use moves whenever the opportunity arises. Even in those moments where you do not immediately see the significance, moves have a way of generating significance on their own. This contract depends on the players knowing when each move could be employed, and which move is best for the moment. No one player owns this job. The MC, fitting with ttrpg traditions, often sits well positioned to make the call, though any other person at the table may contribute all the same, including the player making the move.

In this game, more so than most pbta, the players are encouraged to call out their moves by name and intent. Literally, "I want to do that voodoo that I do, so that I can...". The MC, likewise, is encouraged to call out the moves their NPCs are making at the players' characters. Playing out the action in scene (or alluding to how they do it, via conversation) ensures that you have your character actually _do_ the move, in accordance with the fiction (a part of play that can happen either before or after you call your move, the order matters naught). But calling it can be as essential as doing it, else the others at the table might mistake your intent or, worse, not realize you were trying to invoke a move at all!

## The gaps

All the moves in this text serve to elicit a particular mood. The universe of things your characters could do is so much greater than the list this game admits. Sometimes that means the game isn't interested in what you might try to do. Sometimes that means the interest is there, but not by the approach you've taken.

You will hit a gap between the moves. All players do, eventually. They see a significant moment where the rules do not. Caught in such a situation, what is one to do?

For example: you want to undertake a perilous stunt, a precarious exploit, a death-defying feat! But, how? This game has no move to signify your audacity. Nothing even comes close, because this is a game about feelings, not stunts. When that moment occurs at your table the character can simply do it, or not, with complication or without. Pick the most rational option and carry on the conversation. It's not important to this game.

Another example: you've challenged a rival to a duel. A formal and proper- if still lethal- fight. _Scuffle and Fracas_ is too messy, fitted more for a brawl than a duel. So what's left? Look sideways at this scene. Are you trying to reveal a truth about the world to them?  _Come Right Out with It_. Trying to steal the glory that they think is theirs? _Barge In on Someone_. Maybe you want them to reveal their true selves to all around? _Draw Someone Out_.

When all else fails you can fall back to the conversation. Talk about what's a stake, what you want, and what the other players are interested in. No rule states that the conversation cannot be significant on its own. Moves merely help illuminate the point.

## A tutorial

If you're completely new to moves, this bit here walks through an example move, step by step. The move won't appear elsewhere in this game, it's only here for teaching. Nonetheless it fits with all the tone and structure you'll see from the other moves.

> ### Head Over Heels
> When you **try to make someone fall in love with you**, first the other character gets their say: if they could fall for you, roll; if not, try something else. On 2, they fall head over heels for you. On 1, ask what they require to fall in love with you and they must answer honestly. On a miss, ask them if they want to fall in love with you. If they say yes, ask or tell them what obstacle blocks the way.  If they say no, ask what you did wrong, and they have to answer with the cruellest honesty.

First, what is this move doing? Seems you're trying to woo a lover (or a potential one, at least).

This particular move breaks down into three parts.  Two are traditional (the first and final), with an uncommon bridge in the middle.
1. The trigger: **try to make someone fall in love with you**
2. The approval: first the other character get's their say...
3. The ruling: On a 2, 1, or miss...

#### The Trigger

Triggers are the gateway to every move. Doing as the trigger says sets a character on a path, and the rest of the move merely takes us to the destination that this path aught go.

In this example, our trigger is you attempting to make someone fall in love with you. By and large, that requires you to do exactly what it says: you have to make the effort (not someone on your behalf), and your goal is to make someone fall in love with you (not with anyone else). At the same time what's left unstated is also important. For example, do you have to love them back? Not at all!

#### The Approval

This move has a special case: we have to ask the other character for permission before we continue.

"Approval" isn't a canonical term for this kind of mechanic. It isn't even a canonical mechanic when it comes to moves! Begs the question, why include an outlandish addition to a tutorial example? An elementary lesson: there is no strict rule about what a move can or cannot require. You, the players, must follow the text in each and every move as its own unique directive. 

#### The Ruling

Finally, the rest. By standard you'll find some kind of resolution (a roll, or perhaps vote) or cost, or both.  The rules here may be as simple or as complex as the game requires. Moves are not required to follow any strict format.

This particular move is on the basic side. The player must roll (see: [rolling](/mechanics/rolling.md)), and the results follow the basic trio of outcomes: clear success, complicated success, and a miss. On the best outcome (2), the character wins their love with no strings attached. If it gets complicated, they'll need to put in extra effort to get what they want. In this case their requirement doesn't need to be satisfiable immediately. Or perhaps at all. They lay down the line for you to cross.

The 'miss' case in this move is tightly controlled: the players involved get a bit of choice, but not much. Many other moves leave the outcome wide open, allowing the table to build on the fallout with innumerable variations. What one tends to find across all pbta is the following: you didn't necessarily fail, you're not going to get what you want in the way that you want it, and it's up to the other player to decide what that means. Despite the prescribed possible consequences, this move still holds tight to that formula.

#### Modifying moves

Modification, reinterpretation, and reinvention are all a key part of play, in this game as much as any other environment. Moves are not immune to those impulses. Loose interpretations and rule-bending are allowed, and even encouraged, so long as the intent of the move is honored.

Let's imagine three different attempts to use the example move at a slant. Each of the following are functional, though not necessarily appropriate.

* Suppose you want to make more than one person fall in love with you- an entire audience, let's say- all at once and together! When you **try to make an audience fall in love with you**. This revision doesn't change the tone of the ruling nor the possible ways that we re-evaluate the characters' relationships. I think this variation sounds reasonable!
<br>
<br>
* What if you aren't looking for love, but for devotion? When you **try to make someone devote themselves to you**. People in love are often devoted, though devotion isn't necessary for love. Nor is love necessary for devotion. In result, the move now explores an altogether different territory than before. The rest of the rules still function well enough, perhaps after attending one or two other touch ups, and it's still fundamentally concerned with the characters' ongoing relationship. I wouldn't recommend this variation casually. But if the MC and player sat down, thought it over, and concluded they'd want that character to explore dedication over love, it's a wholly acceptable rewrite.
<br>
<br>
* And if you just want something more physical? When you **try to get someone to have sex with you**. Right away I'll advise against the change. Not because of the content (though this is a prime reminder that every table should use their [safety tools](/playing/safety.md)), but because the original move is designed to investigate the change in a relationship, and this revision focuses on a solitary physical act. Not that we couldn't or shouldn't focus on relationships when we focus on sex; on the contrary! But this move is not equipped to handle such an exploration. It would take much more than a switch to the trigger for the text to support it properly.
